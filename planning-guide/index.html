The Challenge

Most plans die quietly, around week four

Almost every company at your size can produce a plan — two days offsite, a deck, three objectives, a set of key results everyone nods at. Then the quarter starts. Someone asks how you're tracking, and the room goes quiet, because nobody has opened the doc since the day it was written. By March, the plan is a file in a folder and the team is working on whatever came up that week.

The step that gets skipped is translation — turning an objective into projects, projects into tasks with a deliverable and an owner, and putting those tasks in the system your team already uses. That translation is what the guide walks you through, in about 90 minutes.

  • The plan nobody graded — you never find out which key results had no owner, so you just write six new ones and repeat
  • Objectives that are actually projects — "launch the pricing page" is work, not the outcome it's meant to produce
  • The exec team owning everything — leadership in name, a bottleneck in practice, capped at what four busy people can carry
  • Planning as an event — two days of intensity, eleven weeks of silence, same result as not planning at all

What's Inside

The six-step system

Each step is expanded in the guide with the traps that derail it and four worked examples from our own work. Run the whole thing with your leadership team and last quarter's plan in front of you.

  • 01Grade last quarter (15 min): score every key result 0 to 1, then ask who actually worked on it. The count nobody touched is your real capacity signal.
  • 02Three objectives, not seven (10 min): each one a sentence about an outcome, not an activity — what a leadership team can hold while running the company.
  • 03Numbers with owners (20 min): two to four key results per objective, each with a real starting number, a target, and one name — never a team.
  • 04Cluster into projects (20 min): group the key results delivered by the same body of work. Three to five projects for the whole company.
  • 05Break into deliverables (20 min): five to ten tasks per project, each with a thing you can point at when it's done.
  • 06Wire it in (5 min): put every task in the system your team already opens Monday morning, then set the review cadence before anyone leaves the room.

From Our Own Work

Four rooms this exact system ran in

Specific targets, real owners, real numbers — this is what the translation looks like once it leaves the whiteboard.

Healthcare · Hospital GI suite

Targets precise enough to miss

Room turnover from 29 minutes to 15, procedures per room from 10.4 to 13, referral-to-procedure wait from 5 months to 3. They finished at 23.7 minutes, 11.4 procedures, and 2 months — two targets missed, one beaten, and the miss revealed a second constraint they hadn't planned for.

Operations · Warehouse

A plan that enforced itself

Instead of writing the new quality-control policy in a document, the team built it into the app — error rate below 1 percent moved a picker to sampled inspection, past 1.5 percent moved them back, tied to their bonus. The plan didn't depend on a manager remembering it mid-shift.

Logistics · Fulfillment, in crisis

Two tracks, both standard work

Daily manual triage to stop the bleeding, and a systems rebuild so it stopped for good — both written into daily standard work, not a project doc, so the fix would survive the next reorganization. Fill rate went from 75 percent to 99, and stayed there through the following year.

Distribution · Losing its only supplier

Fourteen months of parallel work

Planned backward from a target state, with success metrics defined for each of five scenarios they might face. The business kept running and grew from about $75M to $155M during the transition — the plan held because every piece had an owner, a deliverable, and a date.
