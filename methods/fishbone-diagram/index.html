Fishbone Diagram: What It Is and How to Build One

A fishbone diagram sorts the possible causes of a problem into categories, so a team can see them all at once and decide which to check.

Home › Methods › Fishbone Diagram

By Vincent Matranga, co-founder of Waya · Updated November 17, 2026

A fishbone diagram, also called an Ishikawa or cause-and-effect diagram, is a chart for finding the causes of a problem. The problem sits at the head, and each bone is a category of cause, such as flow, information, people, and technology. A team fills in the bones, then checks the most likely causes against evidence.

What is a fishbone diagram?

A fishbone diagram is a picture of everything that might be causing a problem, grouped by kind. Kaoru Ishikawa introduced it in Japan in the 1940s, and it became one of the basic tools of quality control. It is a way of doing root cause analysis as a group.

Its value is coverage. A team that brainstorms without it tends to stay on the first plausible cause. The bones make the group look at each category in turn, including the ones nobody suspected.

When should you use a fishbone diagram?

Use a fishbone diagram when the problem crosses teams and no single chain of cause and effect explains it. Typical cases:

  • Several departments play a part in the problem.
  • Each department has a different theory of the cause.
  • The problem has persisted for months.
  • The team needs to agree on the causes before anyone will act.

When the problem sits inside one team and the causes form a single chain, 5 Whys is faster.

How do you build a fishbone diagram?

Building a fishbone diagram takes six steps. The first three draw it, and the last three turn it into something you can act on.

  1. Write the problem at the headWhat exactly are we explaining?

    State the problem as a measured fact, such as "first response on support tickets averages 22 hours". Everyone in the room should agree on the wording before any causes go up.

  2. Choose the categoriesWhat kinds of cause could be involved?

    Draw one bone for each category. Use a standard set, or pick four to six that fit the problem.

  3. Fill in the bonesWhy does this happen?

    For each category, ask what in it contributes to the problem. Write each cause on its bone. When a cause has a cause of its own, add it as a smaller bone.

  4. Mark the likely causesWhich of these explain most of the problem?

    Look for causes that show up on more than one bone, and causes the group has seen first-hand. Circle the few that seem to carry the most weight.

  5. Check them against evidenceHow do we know?

    A fishbone diagram is a list of hypotheses. Check each circled cause with data or direct observation before acting on it.

  6. Take the confirmed causes forwardWhat will we try?

    Each confirmed cause becomes an experiment with an expected result and an owner.

Which categories should a fishbone diagram use?

Use the categories that fit the kind of work. The classic set was written for factories, and it fits a software or service company poorly.

6 MsMethods, machines, materials, manpower, measurement, mother nature (environment)Manufacturing and physical operations
4 SsSurroundings, suppliers, systems, skillsService businesses
Operations setFlow, information, people, technologySmall companies, software, and office processes

What does a finished fishbone diagram look like? A worked example

This example uses the causes from a Waya case study on customer support at a food distributor. The team analyzed that case with SIPOC and 5 Whys. Its causes are arranged here as a fishbone to show the layout. The full account is in From a 22-hour to a 6-minute support response.

Problem at the head: first response on support tickets averages 22 hours.

BoneCauses on it
FlowRequests arrive over five disconnected channels. About half are resolved outside any system. A person reads, categorizes, and routes every message. Even a small credit needs escalation.
InformationCustomers cannot say which order or issue a ticket is about. No one can see the real queue. Customers call for delivery times on trucks the company already tracks by GPS.
PeopleOnly 10% of tickets reach the right person automatically. Calls go to voicemail. An executive takes 5 to 10 support calls a day.
TechnologyAgents search several systems to find which warehouse filled an order. The most common questions have no self-service path. Only 10% of requests come through the app.

The case traced these to one root cause: there was no structured front door, so nothing organized a request when it arrived. The fix was an in-app intake that tags each ticket with its order and issue and routes it automatically. First response fell from 22 hours to 6 minutes.

What mistakes do teams make with fishbone diagrams?

  • A vague head. "Poor quality" produces a diagram about everything. Put a measured problem at the head.
  • Forcing the 6 Ms. A support team has little to write under "machines" and "materials". Choose categories that fit.
  • Treating the diagram as the answer. Every item on it is a guess until it is checked.
  • Writing solutions on the bones. "No CRM" is a solution. Write what happens because of it.
  • No prioritizing. Forty causes with equal weight lead nowhere. Circle the few that matter.
  • One person filling it in. The method works because people from different teams see different causes.

How does a fishbone diagram compare with 5 Whys?

Fishbone diagram5 Whys
ShapeWide: many causes across categoriesDeep: one chain, cause by cause
Best forA problem that crosses teamsA problem inside one team
PeopleA cross-functional groupOne to three people
RiskToo many causes and no focusMissing causes outside the chain
OutputHypotheses to checkA root cause to test a fix against

The two work together. The fishbone finds where to look, and 5 Whys goes down each likely bone.

How do you run a fishbone session in a small company?

A small company can build a fishbone diagram in an hour with one person from each function in the room.

  • Four bones are enough. Flow, information, people, and technology cover most problems in a company of under 50 people.
  • Invite the people who touch the work. In a small company that may be three people.
  • Check causes by looking. You can often verify a cause the same day by reading a few tickets or watching the process once.

Can an AI assistant build a fishbone diagram?

An AI assistant can facilitate one. It keeps the problem statement fixed, takes the group through each category in turn, asks what evidence supports each cause, and records the result. It cannot see your process, so the causes and the checks come from the people in the room.

Lean AI is Waya's MCP server that runs fishbone sessions this way inside Claude, as part of its gap analysis.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called a fishbone diagram?

Because of its shape. The problem is written at the right, like the head of a fish. A horizontal line runs back from it like a spine, and the categories of cause branch off it like bones.

Is a fishbone diagram the same as an Ishikawa diagram?

Yes. It is named after Kaoru Ishikawa, the Japanese quality engineer who introduced it in the 1940s. It is also called a cause-and-effect diagram.

What are the 6 Ms in a fishbone diagram?

The 6 Ms are the standard categories for a manufacturing problem: methods, machines, materials, manpower, measurement, and mother nature (the environment). Service and office problems usually fit other categories better, such as flow, information, people, and technology.

When should you use a fishbone diagram instead of 5 Whys?

Use a fishbone diagram when the problem crosses several teams, when each team has its own theory of the cause, or when the problem has been around for a long time. Use 5 Whys when the problem sits in one area and the causes form a single chain.

Can you use a fishbone diagram and 5 Whys together?

Yes. Build the fishbone first to see the range of possible causes, then run 5 Whys down each bone that looks likely until you reach a cause you can act on.
