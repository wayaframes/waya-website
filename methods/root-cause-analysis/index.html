Root Cause Analysis: How to Find the Real Cause

Root cause analysis is a method for tracing a problem back to the cause that, once fixed, stops the problem from coming back.

Home › Methods › Root Cause Analysis

By Vincent Matranga, co-founder of Waya · Updated November 17, 2026

Root cause analysis (RCA) is a structured way to find why a problem happens, so that the fix removes the cause and the problem stays fixed. You state the problem with evidence, trace it back cause by cause with 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram, confirm the root cause with data, and then test a fix.

What is root cause analysis?

Root cause analysis is the work of getting from what is wrong to why it is wrong. A symptom is what you see: late deliveries, a falling margin, a long queue. A root cause is the reason behind it that you can change, and whose removal stops the symptom from coming back.

Most fixes are aimed at symptoms. The loudest complaint gets a patch, the number improves for a month, and then the problem returns because its cause is still in place. Root cause analysis delays the fix until the cause is known.

It is the diagnostic part of a gap analysis, and step 4 of A3 thinking.

When should you use root cause analysis?

Use root cause analysis when a problem has a measurable effect and its cause is not established. Typical cases:

  • The same problem has been fixed before and has come back.
  • People in the company give different explanations for it.
  • The explanation on offer is a person or a team, with no account of why it happened.
  • A fix would be expensive, and you want to know it is aimed at the right thing.

It is not needed when the cause is plain and the fix is cheap. It also cannot start from a vague complaint. If the problem is "communication isn't working", state it as a measured fact first.

What are the steps of a root cause analysis?

A root cause analysis has five steps. The first two set it up, and the last three do the tracing and checking.

  1. State the problemWhat is happening, and how do you know?

    Write one sentence that describes what you observe, with a number and a time frame. "Support is slow" is too vague to analyze. "First response averages 22 hours" can be traced.

  2. Choose the methodDoes the problem sit in one area or cross several?

    A problem inside one team or process suits 5 Whys. A problem that crosses teams, where each has its own theory, suits a fishbone diagram.

  3. Trace the causesWhy does this happen?

    Ask why, and answer with something you can observe. Ask why again of that answer. When an answer has two causes, follow both branches.

  4. Confirm the root causeWould the problem stop if this were removed?

    A root cause passes three checks: there is evidence for it, it is within your control, and removing it would prevent the problem from recurring.

  5. Prioritize and test a fixWhich causes matter most?

    Most problems have more than one root cause. Pick the one or two with the largest effect, write each fix as an experiment, and measure the result.

Which root cause analysis method should you use?

Choose the method by where the problem lives. One area with a traceable chain of events suits 5 Whys. Several teams, each with a theory, suit a fishbone diagram.

MethodBest forWho is neededWhat you get
5 WhysA problem inside one team or process, with a chain of cause and effectOne to three people who know the workOne or more chains from symptom to root cause
Fishbone diagramA problem that crosses teams and has several kinds of causePeople from each team involvedPossible causes sorted by category, to be checked
Pareto analysisMany small causes, with counts availableSomeone with the dataCauses ranked by how often they occur
Fault tree analysisA safety or reliability failure with combined causesEngineers who know the systemA logic tree of the failures that lead to the event

What does a root cause analysis look like? A worked example

This example comes from a GI and endoscopy suite in a regional hospital. The full account is in the case study Cutting a 5-month patient wait to 2.

StageWhat happened
The problemMore than 560 patients waited up to five months for a colonoscopy. Turning a room over took about 29 minutes, which capped each of the two rooms at about 10 procedures a day.
The methodThe team mapped the changeover role by role (physician, techs, two nurses) and ran 5 Whys on the longest delays.
The first answerRoom turnover is slow.
What the whys foundTurnover was only part of it. Often the next case could not start because the physician was covering clinic or on call, or because the patient was not ready: a late arrival, prep or fasting not followed, no nurse free for a difficult IV, no open bed, or missing orders in a records system not built for GI.
The fixesStandard work for room turnover. A standard order set and recovery protocol in the records system. An IV placement priority process and standard prep and fasting screening. A redesigned block schedule.
The resultThe wait from referral to procedure fell from 5 months to 2, against a target of 3. Room turnover fell from 29 minutes to 23.7. Procedures per room per day rose from 10.4 to 11.4.

The analysis began on room turnover and found a second cause, patient readiness. Hitting the wait target took fixes to both.

What mistakes do teams make in root cause analysis?

  • Stopping at the first why. The first answer is usually another symptom. "Orders ship late because picking is slow" still leaves the question of why picking is slow.
  • Stopping at a person. "Human error" ends the analysis one step early. Ask what made the error easy to make.
  • Assuming one cause. Chains branch. Follow each branch that has evidence behind it.
  • Links without evidence. Each "because" should rest on something observed. A chain of guesses leads to a guessed fix.
  • Going past what you control. "The economy" may be true, and you cannot act on it. Stop at the deepest cause you can change.
  • Fixing every cause at once. Five root causes do not need five projects. Start with the one or two that account for most of the gap.

How do you run a root cause analysis in a small company?

A small company can run a root cause analysis in under an hour with the people who do the work. A 5 Whys session takes 30 to 90 minutes.

  • Observation counts as evidence. You may not have a dashboard. Ten recent tickets, read one by one, are enough to support a link in the chain.
  • Go and look. Trace the causes where the work happens, with the person who does it.
  • Stop at good enough to act. The aim is a cause you are confident enough to test a fix against.

Can an AI assistant run a root cause analysis?

An AI assistant can facilitate a root cause analysis. Asked why churn went up, a general assistant will list plausible causes. Given the method, it asks for the evidence behind each answer, follows the branches, and keeps asking why until the cause is one you can act on.

Frequently asked questions

What is a root cause?

A root cause is the underlying reason a problem happens. It is the point in the chain of causes where a change would stop the problem from recurring, and where you have the ability to make that change. Causes above it in the chain are symptoms.

How many times should you ask why?

Five is a guide. Stop when you reach a cause that you can act on and whose removal would prevent the problem from returning. Some chains get there in three questions, and some take seven.

Can a problem have more than one root cause?

Yes, and most do. In a hospital GI suite Waya worked with, slow room turnover was one cause of a five-month wait and patients not being ready was another. Hitting the target meant fixing both. Work on the one or two causes with the largest effect first.

Is human error a root cause?

No. "Someone made a mistake" is where the analysis starts. Ask what in the process made the mistake easy to make or hard to catch. A root cause is something in the process, the information, or the system that you can change.

Which tools are used for root cause analysis?

The two most common are 5 Whys, for a problem inside one area, and the fishbone diagram, for a problem that crosses several teams. Pareto analysis ranks causes by frequency, and fault tree analysis maps the combinations of failures behind a safety or reliability event.

Related methods

  • A3 thinking: the seven-step method that frames the whole problem, from background to what was learned.
  • Gap analysis: stating the problem, choosing the method, and finding why the gap exists.
  • Fishbone diagram: sorting the possible causes of a cross-team problem into categories.
