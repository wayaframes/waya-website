A3 Thinking: What It Is and How to Run One

A3 thinking is a seven-step problem-solving method from Toyota that fits a whole problem, from the evidence to the fix, on one sheet of paper.

Home › Methods › A3 Thinking

By Vincent Matranga, co-founder of Waya · Updated November 17, 2026

A3 thinking is a structured problem-solving method, developed at Toyota, that fits a whole problem on one sheet of A3 paper. It moves through seven steps: the background, the measured current state, the target, the root causes, the experiments, the action plan, and what was learned. Each fix is treated as a test, following the Plan-Do-Study-Adjust cycle.

What is A3 thinking?

A3 thinking is the scientific method applied to a business problem. You state the problem with numbers, find out why it happens before proposing a fix, test the fix, and check whether the number moved.

The name comes from the paper. A3 is 297 by 420 mm, close to 11 by 17 inches, and Toyota used one sheet of it to report on a problem. The limit forces clarity. If the problem, the analysis, and the plan do not fit on one page, the thinking is not finished.

The sheet is the record. The method is the reasoning behind it. John Shook, who learned it as a manager at Toyota, describes the A3 in Managing to Learn as a way to make problem solving visible so that others can question it and learn from it.

When should you use A3 thinking?

Use A3 thinking when a result is off target, you can measure the gap, and you do not yet know the cause. Typical cases:

  • A problem keeps coming back after it was "fixed".
  • Improvement work is under way and the main number is not moving.
  • Several people disagree about what the problem is.
  • A decision needs a diagnosis that will still hold up in six months.

Skip the full method when the cause is obvious and the fix is cheap. Do it and check the result. And when the whole system has to be redesigned because improving the current one cannot close the gap, the A3 will show that in step 4. That is a job for Kaikaku, a radical redesign, with the A3 as the frame around it.

What are the seven steps of an A3?

An A3 has seven steps. The first four define and diagnose the problem. The last three test and keep the fix.

  1. BackgroundWhat is the problem, and why does it matter now?

    State the problem in one sentence, grounded in what you can observe. Say what is in scope and what is out. Name the constraints that will not change.

  2. Current conditionsWhere are we now?

    Measure today's state: the numbers, and how the work actually flows. If nobody knows the current number, that is a finding, and getting it is the first action.

  3. Target conditionsWhere do we need to be?

    Set a specific target for each baseline number, with a date. "Improve margin" is not a target. "Raise gross margin from 20% to 50% by June" is.

  4. Gap analysisWhy does the gap exist?

    Trace each symptom to a root cause backed by evidence. 5 Whys suits a problem in one area. A fishbone diagram suits one with causes in several.

  5. ExperimentsWhat will we try?

    For each root cause, write a hypothesis: if we do this, we expect that result, because of this reasoning. Start with the smallest version you can test.

  6. Action planWho does what, by when?

    Every action gets one named owner and a due date. Keep the status where everyone can see it.

  7. Study and reflectWhat did we learn?

    Compare the result with what you expected, as early as you can measure it. Keep what worked as the new standard. Take what you learned into the next cycle.

The steps are a progression, and the time spent on each varies. A poorly understood problem can take most of its time in steps 1 to 4.

What does a finished A3 look like? A worked example

This example comes from a company that processed supplier invoices for independent restaurants. Over seven months in 2016 its gross margin rose from about 20% to about 70%. The full account is in the case study From 20% to 70% gross margin.

StepWhat went on the page
1. BackgroundAn invoice with line items cost as much to process as it brought in. Line-item extraction could not be offered to every customer.
2. Current conditionsMarch 2016: $1.67 per invoice to process, plus $1.67 for line-item extraction, $3.34 in total. Extraction took 24 hours. Gross margin near 20%.
3. Target conditionsExtraction at $0.80 per invoice with a 4-hour turnaround.
4. Gap analysisEvery line item was typed by hand twice, then checked line by line. Invoices moved in batches of 25 to 200. Only one person on one shift was trained to verify them.
5. ExperimentsBuild extraction into the app so it fills in the products and units it already knows. Move invoices one at a time, first in, first out. Train the night shift to verify.
6. Action planExtraction went live in July 2016. A second outsourced team and 3 of 5 US contractors were wound down.
7. Study and reflectCost per invoice was tracked for 90 days after launch. All-in cost fell from $3.34 to $0.97, turnaround from 24 hours to under 30 minutes, and monthly volume grew from 9,663 to 14,485 invoices.

Extraction ended at $0.41 per invoice against the $0.80 target. The gap analysis had found two causes, double entry and batching, and the fixes removed both.

What mistakes do teams make with A3s?

  • Starting from the solution. "We need a new system" is a fix, and no problem has been stated yet. Ask what the system would change, and write that down as the problem.
  • No baseline. A target without a current number cannot be checked. Measure first.
  • Symptoms listed as causes. "Invoices are slow" restates the problem. "Invoices wait in batches of 200" is a cause you can act on.
  • Bundling problems. Three problems on one page get three shallow analyses. Pick the one with the largest effect.
  • Blame. "The team doesn't follow the process" stops the analysis. Ask what in the process makes the wrong step the easy one.
  • Stopping at step 6. An action plan with no follow-up measurement is a to-do list. Step 7 is where you find out whether you were right.
  • Treating the form as the goal. A filled-in template with no evidence behind it is paperwork.

How does A3 thinking compare with 8D and DMAIC?

A3, 8D, and DMAIC all go from a defined problem to a verified fix. They differ in origin, weight, and the kind of problem they suit.

OriginToyotaFordSix Sigma (Motorola, GE)

Best forAny gap between a current and a target resultA defect or customer complaint that needs containing firstReducing variation where there is enough data for statistics

Who runs itWhoever owns the problemA cross-functional teamA trained project lead

How do you run an A3 in a small company?

A small company can run an A3 with one person and an hour. Three things change from the factory version.

  • The data is thinner. A 15-person company rarely has a tracked baseline. A count from last week's tickets or invoices is enough to start.
  • The owner and the analyst are the same person. Write the A3 anyway and show it to one other person. Its purpose is to let someone else question your reasoning.
  • Experiments are faster. You can often test a change the same week. Measure at the earliest point you can.

Can an AI assistant run an A3?

An AI assistant can facilitate an A3. It cannot do the observing for you. Asked for help with a problem, a general assistant will suggest fixes straight away, before anyone knows the cause. Given the method, it asks the seven questions in order, pushes back when a solution arrives before a cause, and writes up the page.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called A3?

A3 is a paper size, 297 by 420 mm, close to 11 by 17 inches. Toyota used a single sheet of it to report on a problem. The limit is the point: if the problem, the analysis, and the plan do not fit on one sheet, the thinking is not finished.

What is the difference between an A3 report and A3 thinking?

The A3 report is the one-page document. A3 thinking is the reasoning that produces it: measure the current state, set a target, find the root cause, and test a fix. A completed form with no evidence behind it is an A3 report without A3 thinking.

How long does an A3 take?

It depends on the problem. A clear problem with an identifiable cause takes 15 to 30 minutes. A problem that needs investigation and root cause analysis takes one to four hours. A problem that crosses several teams can run for weeks, with the A3 updated as results come in.

Is A3 thinking only for manufacturing?

No. It works on any problem with a measurable gap. Waya has used it on gross margin, working capital, support response time, and patient wait times in software, food distribution, and a hospital.
